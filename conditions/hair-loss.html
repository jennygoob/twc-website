Hair Loss

Your Hair Is Talking. Your Hormones Are the Translator.

The shower drain. The thinning part. The receding line you keep telling yourself is lighting. Hair loss is one of the most visible and most distressing symptoms of hormonal imbalance — and one of the most responsive to treatment when the right cause is identified. The pattern tells the story. The bloodwork confirms it.

Read Your Pattern

Different Patterns. Different Causes.

Hair loss is not one condition. It is a family of patterns — each pointing to a specific hormonal or nutritional driver. Knowing your pattern narrows the search. Labs confirm the cause.

Androgenetic Pattern

Crown and Temples (Men) / Part Widening (Women)

Driven by DHT — a potent testosterone metabolite that miniaturizes hair follicles over time. The pattern is predictable: temples and crown in men, diffuse thinning along the part in women. Responds to DHT modulation and hormonal optimization.

Key markers: DHT, Free Testosterone, SHBG
Thyroid Pattern

Diffuse, Uniform Thinning

Thyroid dysfunction disrupts the hair growth cycle itself — pushing more follicles into resting (telogen) phase simultaneously. The result is uniform thinning across the entire scalp, not a localized pattern. Often accompanied by dry, brittle texture.

Key markers: TSH, Free T3, Free T4, Thyroid antibodies
Stress / Telogen Effluvium

Sudden, Excessive Shedding

Acute or chronic cortisol elevation pushes a disproportionate number of follicles into shedding phase. The shedding often occurs 2-3 months after the stressor — illness, surgery, emotional trauma, crash dieting — making the cause difficult to trace without testing.

Key markers: Cortisol, DHEA-S, Ferritin
Nutrient Deficiency

Slow Growth, Breakage, Thin Shafts

Hair is metabolically expensive. When iron, ferritin, biotin, zinc, or vitamin D are depleted, the body diverts resources to survival functions. Hair production is deprioritized — follicles produce thinner shafts, grow more slowly, and break more easily.

Key markers: Ferritin, Iron, Vitamin D, Zinc, B12
Beyond the Surface

The Hormonal Drivers Your Dermatologist Never Tested

Standard hair loss treatment starts with topical solutions and finasteride. Those address one mechanism — DHT at the follicle. But hair loss driven by thyroid dysfunction, iron deficiency, cortisol elevation, or estrogen decline will not respond to topical treatment. The cause is systemic. The solution must be too.

  • DHT / Testosterone

    Follicle Miniaturization

    Dihydrotestosterone binds to follicle receptors, progressively shrinking them. Genetic sensitivity determines which follicles are vulnerable. Modulating DHT levels slows and often reverses the process.

  • Thyroid

    Growth Cycle Disruption

    Both hypo- and hyperthyroidism disrupt the anagen-to-telogen transition. Too little thyroid hormone slows growth. Too much accelerates shedding. The full thyroid cascade reveals the pattern.

  • Iron / Ferritin

    Follicle Starvation

    Ferritin below 40 ng/mL — technically within normal range — is strongly associated with hair loss. The follicle's oxygen and energy supply depends on adequate iron stores.

  • Cortisol

    Stress-Induced Shedding

    Elevated cortisol shifts hair follicles from growth phase to resting phase prematurely. Chronic stress produces chronic shedding that no topical treatment can override.

  • Estrogen

    Hormonal Transition Thinning

    Estrogen supports the anagen (growth) phase. Perimenopause, postpartum, and PCOS-related estrogen fluctuations produce diffuse thinning that correlates precisely with hormonal shift timing.
